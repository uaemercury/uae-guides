Asking someone to say more sounds simple, yet "Can you elaborate?" produces very different reactions depending on who reads it and where. The questions below are the ones people actually have about this small, common phrase — answered plainly.

Is "Can you elaborate?" rude?

Not inherently — but it is bare. Without a subject or a softener, it can read as a challenge, especially in writing, where the reader cannot hear your tone. "Could you elaborate on the pricing?" is the same request with the risk removed. In speech, with a friendly voice, the plain version is perfectly fine.

What is the most polite way to ask?

"Would you mind elaborating on that?" and "I would appreciate it if you could expand on…" sit at the top of the politeness scale. Both work with clients and senior colleagues. Interestingly, specificity is itself a form of politeness: a precise question respects the other person's time more than a vague one does.

What can I say instead in a meeting?

Meetings reward conversational phrasing: "Could you walk us through that?" for a process, "What do you mean by…?" for a term, "Can you give us an example?" for an abstract claim, and "Tell me more about that" when you simply want someone to keep going. A fuller list of other ways to say can you elaborate groups these by situation so you can pick by setting rather than by instinct.

Is "elaborate" a formal word?

Yes, mildly. It comes from Latin roots meaning "worked out," and it still carries a slight academic and professional flavour. That is why it feels natural in a report or an interview and slightly stiff in a chat message, where "unpack," "tell me more" or "how so?" do the same job in modern dress.

What is the difference between "elaborate" and "clarify"?

They solve different problems. Elaborate means "add more" — the answer was too short. Clarify means "make it understandable" — the answer was confusing. Asking for elaboration when you need clarification gets you a longer version of the thing you already didn't understand, which helps no one.

How do I ask a senior person without sounding challenging?

Lead with appreciation, then aim narrowly: "That's helpful — could you expand a little on how the timeline was set?" The acknowledgement proves you listened; the narrow target proves you are not questioning the whole piece of work. Senior readers answer narrow questions quickly because narrow questions are cheap to answer.

Can I just write "Elaborate?" in an email?

You can, and people do — but it is the riskiest form of the request. One word, no subject, no softener: many readers will hear dissatisfaction in it. If brevity matters, "Could you elaborate on the costs?" is only four words longer and carries none of the risk.

The pattern behind every answer above is the same: the phrase is fine, the aim is everything. Say what you want more of, and almost any wording will work.

How should I respond when someone asks me to elaborate?

Briefly, in structure, and starting with the headline. The person asking usually wants the reasoning or the missing layer, not a repeat of everything at greater length. A reliable pattern: restate your point in one sentence, add the two or three details that support it, and finish with the implication — "so the practical effect is…" If the request was vague, it is also acceptable, and often wise, to ask which part they want expanded before writing at length. Answering an elaboration request well is a visible skill: managers notice the people whose expansions are organised, and they route the important questions to them again.
