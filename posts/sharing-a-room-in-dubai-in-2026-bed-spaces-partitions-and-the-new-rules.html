Sharing a Room in Dubai in 2026: Bed Spaces, Partitions and the New Rules

Sharing a flat with strangers is the most common way to keep living costs down in Dubai. But since 26 August 2026, shared housing has been permit-only under Law No. 4 of 2026, and renting from the wrong person can end with a municipal fine — or an eviction notice.

This guide covers the three sharing options honestly: what a bed space, a partition and a private room actually cost in 2026, which areas are cheapest, what the new law requires, and the five checks that separate a legal rental from a risky one.

Bed space, partition and private room: what the words mean

Dubai's shared-rental market uses three terms that sound similar but mean very different things — and the 2026 law treats them differently too.

A bed space is one bed in a shared room, usually shared with four to eight people, sometimes in bunk beds. Kitchen, bathroom and living areas are shared, and utilities — DEWA, Wi-Fi and air conditioning — are normally included. It is the cheapest way to live in Dubai, and the option the new law regulates most closely.

A partition is a section of a room divided off with boards to create a semi-private sleeping space for one or two people. It costs more than a bed space and gives you more privacy, but the partition must meet fire-safety and build standards — wooden or non-fire-rated gypsum boards are banned outright.

A private room is a full lockable bedroom inside a shared apartment, sometimes with an attached bathroom. You share the kitchen and living room, and the price is typically double or more than a bed space.

What does a bed space in Dubai cost in 2026?

Across the city, a standard bed space runs from AED 330 to AED 1,100 a month, with most advertised prices including utilities. The area matters enormously — Deira, Al Quoz and International City are the cheapest, while Dubai Marina and Downtown cost two to three times more. This guide to bed space in Dubai breaks down typical 2026 rates in detail.

As a quick overview:

  • Deira (Al Rigga, Naif, Al Muraqqabat): AED 450–900 — the cheapest; near the metro and old markets.
  • Bur Dubai (Al Raffa, Al Fahidi): AED 600–1,000 — close to Deira and Sheikh Zayed Road jobs.
  • Al Quoz: AED 500–800 — popular near industrial zones and warehouses.
  • Karama: AED 700–1,100 — popular with retail workers.
  • Al Nahda: AED 500–1,100 — near the Sharjah border with metro access.
  • International City: AED 500–850 — budget hub with a large South Asian community.
  • Dubai Marina / JBR: AED 1,000–2,000 — premium areas with small, often crowded rooms.

Three things push the price up: fewer occupants, proximity to a metro station, and furnished rooms. A bed in a three-person room near a metro can cost twice what a bunk in a six-person room in the same neighbourhood costs.

The cheapest listings in Deira (Naif, Al Murar, Al Rigga) and International City start around AED 330–500 a month — usually bunks in rooms of six to ten people, and the most likely to be illegal partitions or overcrowded units. A single bed space, with only one or two other people in the room, costs AED 1,000–1,450 a month in Bur Dubai or Karama.

The 2026 law changed the rules

Dubai's Law No. 4 of 2026 came into force on 26 August 2026 — the first law to give shared housing a proper permit system. The points that matter to renters:

  • A permit is mandatory. No residential unit may be used for shared housing without a permit from Dubai Municipality, issued in coordination with the Dubai Land Department.
  • Only owners or authorised establishments may lease. A tenant who rents a flat and then sublets rooms, partitions or bed spaces to others is explicitly prohibited — the old "one master tenant, ten partitions" model no longer survives this law.
  • Minimum space per resident. Around 5 square metres of net living area per person, counted only on bedrooms and living rooms. Kitchens, bathrooms, balconies, corridors, storage rooms and parking areas never count as sleeping space.
  • Partition standards. Timber and non-fire-rated gypsum board partitions are banned, as is anything blocking the route from a bed to the front door or fire stairs.
  • Fines are serious. Violations carry fines from AED 500 to AED 500,000, doubled — up to AED 1 million — if repeated within a year. Fines land on whoever rents the unit out; the tenant risks eviction.

Enforcement is not theoretical: in August 2026, tenants were evicted from the Toyota Building in Naif after flats housing as many as 15 people were found. Existing operators have until 26 August 2027 to comply, but do not rent into a unit that is waiting out the clock.

Five checks before you pay a deposit

Run through these checks before handing over any money:

  1. Ask who the landlord is. Only the property owner or a licensed, authorised establishment may lease shared housing. If the person showing you the room is a tenant subletting, walk away.
  2. Ask to see the shared-housing permit. An evasive answer is a red flag.
  3. Count the heads. Divide the bedrooms and living rooms by roughly 5 square metres per person. If 12 people are sleeping in a two-bedroom flat, it cannot be compliant.
  4. Look at the partitions. Wooden or thin gypsum-board dividers, rooms carved out of the balcony or kitchen, or beds blocking the front door all signal a violation.
  5. Check the tenancy registration. Ask for written proof of the Ejari tenancy registration and never pay rent without a written receipt.

Also visit in person at a busy hour, check ventilation, fire exits and smoke detectors, photograph the room on move-in day, and save the operator's contact details and a copy of any ID or trade licence they show you.

If a bed space is not for you

Shared flats with three to four people often cost AED 999–1,150 in Deira and offer a calmer setup. Monthly serviced studios start around AED 4,300–5,250 with utilities and cleaning included, which can beat a private room in the Marina. And if the commute works, Sharjah near the Dubai border offers bed spaces from around AED 400.

The bottom line: price is no longer the only thing that matters. Rent from the owner or an authorised operator, insist on a permitted unit, and do your five checks — then the cheap bed becomes a genuinely good deal rather than a risk.
