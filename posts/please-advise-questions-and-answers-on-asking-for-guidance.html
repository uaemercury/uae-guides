Few phrases generate as many quiet questions as "please advise." People type it daily, then wonder whether it sounded rude, whether it was grammatical, and why the reply never came. Here are the questions that come up again and again — with straight answers.

Is "please advise" rude?

It can sound that way, though it rarely is meant that way. The phrase is very short, and in writing, short often reads as curt. Appended to a problem report — "The payment failed again. Please advise." — many readers hear impatience. It is not the words themselves; it is the absence of everything around them. One softening clause ("Please let me know how you'd like to handle this") removes the impression entirely.

Is it grammatically correct?

Yes — "advise" is a verb and "please advise" is a valid imperative, the same family as "please confirm" or "please note." The common objection is not grammar but completeness: advise whom, about what? Formal and legal writing accepts the ellipsis because the document supplies the context. A standalone email often does not. Note the spelling trap, too: "advise" is the verb; "advice" is the noun. "Please advice" is simply wrong.

Why do people use it so much?

Because it feels efficient and safely formal. It is heavily used in international business English and in industries — shipping, banking, law, procurement — where formulaic closings are normal. Writers reach for it as a general-purpose "over to you." The trouble is that a phrase meaning everything ends up requesting nothing in particular, which is why a fuller list of other ways to say please advise sorts the alternatives by the specific thing you need back.

What should I write when I need a decision?

Say that: "Please let me know how you'd like to proceed," or "Could you confirm whether we should go ahead?" Decision-shaped questions get decisions. "Advise" asks for counsel, and counsel is not always what the sender wants — sometimes they want a signature, and the mismatch costs a day.

What if I need an opinion, not a decision?

Ask for one directly: "I'd value your thoughts on this," or "What do you recommend?" These are warmer than "advise" and more precise — they invite judgement rather than instruction, which is usually the real request hiding inside the phrase.

Is it acceptable in formal letters?

Yes. In legal, banking and official correspondence the clipped form is a living convention, and readers in those genres parse it without friction. Context decides: the same two words that are standard in a solicitor's letter can feel abrupt in a friendly project email.

What is the single best replacement?

For everyday email, "Please let me know…" followed by the actual question covers most cases: let me know what you think, how to proceed, whether you agree. It keeps the politeness, keeps the brevity, and — unlike the original — tells the reader exactly what a good reply looks like.

Does it matter who I am writing to?

More than any other factor. Downward and across — to juniors and peers — the clipped form is usually read as efficient. Upward, to a senior decision-maker, it can read as an instruction issued in the wrong direction, and to clients it risks sounding like a demand from a supplier they are paying. The safest habit is to scale the softening to the distance: the less you know the reader, and the higher they sit, the more of the sentence you should write out. "I'd appreciate your advice on…" has never offended anyone in either direction, which is more than can be said for the two-word original at either end of the hierarchy.
