It appears in job posts, emails, texts and task lists — "as soon as possible," four words that pretend to be a deadline. The questions people ask about it tend to be the same handful, and the answers explain most of the low-grade timing stress in office life.

Does "as soon as possible" actually mean today?

Not reliably. Studies and surveys of workplace communication repeatedly find that senders usually mean "today" or "immediately," while receivers commonly read it as "when I can fit it in" — a gap of one to three days, with both sides convinced the meaning was obvious. The phrase carries intent, not information. If today is what you mean, "by end of day today" is what to write.

Is ASAP rude?

In speech, rarely — tone does the softening. In writing, it depends on frequency and framing. A single "could you send this ASAP?" is unremarkable. A stream of ASAPs, especially in subject lines or capitals, reads as pressure without consideration, and people quietly begin to deprioritise the sender. The phrase is best treated like a strong spice: effective in small, deliberate doses.

What is the most polite way to ask for something quickly?

Pair the deadline with the reason: "Could you send the figures by Thursday? The client deck goes out Friday morning." Politeness here is not cushioning — it is information. A reader who knows the when and the why can plan, and planning is what gets your request done. A graded list of other ways to say as soon as possible, from "no rush at all" to "time-critical," lets you pick the pressure level deliberately instead of defaulting to maximum.

What should I write if there is no deadline?

Say so — it is rarer and more welcome than people expect. "At your earliest convenience," "whenever you get a chance" and "no rush, next week is fine" all convert a task from a demand into a background item the reader can schedule calmly. Paradoxically, relaxed requests are often done early, because they are the pleasant ones in the inbox.

Is "ASAP" acceptable in formal writing?

Generally no. Formal correspondence uses "at your earliest convenience" for open timing and a written date — "by close of business on Thursday" — for fixed timing. The acronym belongs to internal, informal communication.

How do I reply when someone sends me an ASAP I can't meet?

Answer the ambiguity, not the pressure: "I can have it to you by Wednesday — will that work, or is Tuesday essential?" You have converted their vague urgency into a concrete negotiation, which is the only form in which urgency can actually be managed.

Why does everyone keep using it, then?

Because it feels like a compromise — firm but not bossy, urgent but not rude. The compromise is an illusion: it outsources the hard part, choosing a real time, to the reader. The writers whose requests land fastest are not the ones who push hardest. They are the ones whose messages contain a clock anyone can read.

Where did "as soon as possible" come from?

The phrase is older than the office. It appears in formal and military correspondence from the nineteenth century, where — crucially — it sat inside letters that also contained real dates, orders and consequences. The twentieth century's business shorthand trimmed it to "ASAP," and the digital era completed the journey: a phrase that once accompanied precise instructions became a substitute for them. Its cousin "at your earliest convenience" travelled the opposite path, keeping its full formal dress. The history explains the modern confusion: ASAP inherited the authority of the old letters without inheriting their precision. Restoring the date to the request is, in a sense, simply finishing what the phrase's original users always wrote.
