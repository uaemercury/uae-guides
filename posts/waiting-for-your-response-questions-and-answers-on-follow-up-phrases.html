It is the closing line of a million emails, and the source of a familiar set of worries: does it sound pushy? Is there a more professional version? How long should you actually wait before writing it? The questions below cover the phrase from every side.

Is "waiting for your response" rude?

Not intentionally — but it is one of the closings most likely to be read that way. It focuses on your state (waiting) rather than the reader's, and in writing it can carry a tapping-foot quality, particularly when the message it closes is short. The safer classics — "I look forward to hearing from you" — express the identical expectation in the grammar of anticipation, and cost nothing to use instead.

What is the difference between "waiting for" and "looking forward to"?

Meaning: almost none. Posture: everything. "Waiting" positions the reader as the cause of a delay; "looking forward" positions the reply as something pleasant approaching. Formal English made this choice centuries ago, which is why letters have "looked forward" ever since. When in doubt, choose the version that describes the reply as welcome rather than overdue.

How do I say it when I genuinely need a fast reply?

Add the two things the phrase lacks — a date and a reason: "Could you reply by Thursday? The venue needs final numbers on Friday." Readers act on dates and reasons. A full scale of other ways to say waiting for your response, from patient to pressing, exists because the right pressure depends entirely on which of those two ingredients you can honestly supply.

How long should I wait before following up?

For ordinary business email, two to three working days is the accepted interval; a week for anything non-urgent or addressed to a very senior person. Following up inside a day — unless a stated deadline has passed — reads as chasing, and chasing spends goodwill you may need later. When you do follow up, add value: recap what is pending and shrink the ask ("a quick yes on the budget line is all I need").

Is it acceptable in a first email?

It is the weakest place to use it. Nothing has been waited for yet, so the phrase starts the correspondence with unearned impatience. First messages should close with an invitation — "Let me know what you think" — and reserve follow-up language for an actual follow-up.

What should I write to a senior person or a client?

The deferential forms: "Whenever you have a moment, I'd appreciate your thoughts," or "I look forward to hearing from you at your convenience." Upward and outward, the reader's schedule is formally sovereign; acknowledging that is not weakness, it is the etiquette that gets replies from busy people.

What is the single best default?

"I look forward to hearing from you." It has closed business letters for generations because it does everything at once: expects a reply, implies a relationship, pressures nobody. Default to it, escalate to a dated request when a real deadline exists, and retire "waiting" to the one place it belongs — describing, in the body of a message, a fact: "While we wait for the signed copy, the team will prepare the draft." There, it informs. At the end of a message, it only hovers.

What if the other person writes it to me?

Read it charitably. In much of the world's business English, "waiting for your response" is a routine, unloaded closing — the writer is very likely not tapping any foot. Reply as you would to the polite version: answer the substance, and if a date is impossible, say which date is possible. Modelling the better form is also quietly effective; correspondents absorb each other's habits, and threads that begin with announced waiting often end, a few exchanges later, in "looking forward to hearing from you." Phrases are contagious in both directions — which is the most hopeful fact in this entire subject.
